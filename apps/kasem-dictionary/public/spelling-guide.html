Writing Kasem

The spelling rules and examples below come from Kasem Orthography: Spelling Rules, Kasem Language Committee, Bureau of Ghana Languages, 1997, using the supplied corrected document. They describe Ghana Kasem writing.

a e i o u ɛ ɔ

These are the seven written vowels. The additional written consonant ŋ and the digraphs ch and ny are available in the Kasem keyboard. Phonetic symbols in pronunciation descriptions are not additional letters to use in ordinary spelling.

Use the dictionary to search the book’s words. Open a rule to see how it works. Tone is marked only for the distinctions described below.

Spelling rules

The written Kasem alphabet

Use a, e, i, o, u, ɛ and ɔ as the seven written vowel symbols. Consonant symbols are b, ch, d, f, g, j, k, l, m, n, ny, ŋ, p, r, s, t, v, w, y, z and h (the book brackets h). Use ŋ instead of the old ng symbol, j instead of dʒ, and ch instead of tʃ. The book rejects ɣ. The sounds written phonetically as ɩ, ʋ and ə do not require those letters in ordinary writing.

Source: PDF pages 13.

Seven symbols represent ten vowel sounds

Write /a/ as a, /ɛ/ as ɛ, /i/ as i, /u/ as u and /ɔ/ as ɔ. Write /ɩ/ as e and /ʋ/ as o. Write /e/ as ei and /o/ as wo, subject to the rules for long vowels and repeated vowels. Phonetic spellings are pronunciation descriptions, not replacement headwords.

Writing the central vowel

For /ɜ/ or [ə], write ei in a one-syllable consonant-vowel word; write oa after a labialised consonant in a one-syllable word; write ei in the first syllable of a two-syllable word; write a in the last syllable of a word with two or more syllables. This depends on the word's sound and syllable position, so a global replacement of ə is insufficient.

Keep different vowel sounds distinguishable

The revised spelling distinguishes words that older conventions wrote alike: doŋo is new and dwoŋo is old; pogo is husk and pwogo is nest; kogo is feather, kwogo is zombi and kugu is maize cob. Do not merge words because an older spelling or an ASCII search form looks alike.

Source: PDF pages 16–18.

Write vowel length by doubling

Double the vowel symbol for a long vowel: daa, bii, lɔɔ and tuu. Long /o/ is woo. Long /e/ is ee rather than eei; the book writes teeni for trees and lwooru for beggar.

Vowel digraphs in words with several syllables

When /o/ occurs several times in a word, only its first occurrence is wo: bworo and kwolo. When /e/ occurs several times, only its final occurrence is ei: lelei. These rules depend on the vowel sounds, not on blindly replacing every written e or o.

Include w for labialised consonants

Represent consonant labialisation with w, rather than inserting u and making a one-syllable word look like two syllables. Write words fully even when speech reduces them

Write the full word rather than its fast-speech reduction. This makes different words easier to distinguish. Keep internal vowels as well: write dindeilimi for tongue, lɔgese, tigisi and zamese rather than the shortened pronunciations.

Nouns and determiners are separate words

Spell both the noun and its determiner fully and separately even when they merge in pronunciation. The book gives naane dem, chechare dem and yeili dem. Determiners must not be described as inseparable pieces of every noun.

Verbs and ordinary objects are separate words

Keep verbs fully spelt and separate from ordinary noun objects, including Kale naane and pare tampɔgɔ. With object pronouns such as o and m, spell the verb fully and attach the pronoun using a hyphen: zeili-o, mage-o, bwei-o and bwoi-o.

Preserve the full forms of nasal-final pronunciations

Some words genuinely end in m, including dɔɔm, diim, kam, kom, bam, sem, yeim and zem. Other words only sound shortened in speech: write lane, bane, ywoni, saleme and badwoŋi fully. Keep the final vowel in saŋe, vaŋe, jwoŋi and tɔŋe.

Source: PDF pages 22.

Word division

Write nouns separately from determiners, subject pronouns separately from verbs, verbs separately from ordinary objects, and numerals as independent words. Tense and aspect markers are also separate words. Object pronouns attached by a hyphen are the stated verb-object exception.

Source: PDF pages 23–25.

Noun and adjective compounds use a hyphen

When an adjective qualifies a noun, the noun loses its suffix and the adjective keeps its full form. Hyphenate the compound: nɔn-kukula, na-nia, ka-jworo and na-digiru. Spacing or joining everything without a hyphen can obscure the meaning.

Verb and deverbative compounds

Noun-verb or verb-noun compounds can be written as one word, with or without a hyphen. The book pairs nɔntora with nɔn-tora, golesɔla with gole-sɔla, kadiri with ka-diri and kedia with ke-dia.

A sentence used as a personal name is written as a single name: Ajegedawe. For other clauses or phrases functioning as words, separate constituents with hyphens: Ka-nyɔ-na-ka-ve-muni and Ka-yiŋi-daani.

Write opaque compounds together

When a compound has a meaning different from the ordinary combination of its parts, write it together. Manlaataŋa means rainbow; manlaa taŋa means the chameleon's bow. Digabu and tiabu mean cat; diga bu means one's child.

Use diacritics only in the specified cases

Do not use nasal tildes or vowel-quality diacritics. Ordinary words do not need every tone marked. The book prescribes a limited set of tone distinctions: á, dé, wó, yé and deém, with occasional véi/vèi distinctions. The book provides acute and grave marks, not a general mid-tone macron.

Source: PDF pages 26–29.

Distinguish plural á and dé from a and de

Write á for second-person plural subject you, and a for first-person singular I. Write dé for first-person plural we; write de without that mark for the class II pronoun. The pronoun appendix has conflicting forms; these explicit tone rules control the keyboard shortcuts.

Distinguish positive wó from negative wo

Use wó for the positive future marker and wo for the negative marker. They occur in the same position, so the written tone distinguishes their meanings.

KasemMeaning
Adoa wó yìAdoa will arrive there
Adoa wo yiAdoa did not arrive there

Distinguish yé from ye

Write yé for the negative imperative and ye for the negative subjunctive. The book distinguishes saying that someone should not eat from commanding them not to eat.

Future negative baa has a long vowel

Write the future negative as baa, without a required tone mark; its doubled vowel distinguishes it from imperfective negative ba.

The source's conditional na examples are ambiguous

The book discusses conditional na and a subordinator pronounced ná, but tells the reader to write both as na and prints identical Kasem examples with different English meanings. Preserve this limitation; this document alone does not settle how to distinguish the two written clauses.

The identical source examples are retained in the reference, with an ambiguity notice. They are not presented as unambiguous sentence translations.

Long ago deém differs from last year deem

Write deém for long ago and deem for last year.

KasemMeaning

Occasional tone distinctions on vei

When necessary, write véi for the imperative go, vèi for the past went, and vei for going or intending to go. These are specified distinctions, not permission to place arbitrary accents on every vowel.

KasemMeaning
Adoa, véi yagaAdoa, go to market!
Adoa vèi yagaAdoa went to market
Adoa vei yagaAdoa is going or intends to go to market.

Source: PDF pages 29.

Counting forms and class agreement

The book lists cardinal counting forms kalo/dedoa, nle, ntɔ, nna, nnu, ndo, npae, nana, nogobo/nogo and fuga. Its ordinal/agreement table combines singular de-, ka-, ko- with -doa, -dwe, -doo and plural ba-, ya-, sɛ-, te-, de- with -lei, -tɔ and -na by noun class. Multiplicative forms include bedwe, bilei, betɔ, bena, binu, beredo and nerepae (berepɛ). Keep the table's labels and class scope rather than assuming one universal form per number.
